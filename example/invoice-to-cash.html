I do not know what your invoicing problem actually is. You told me it is ongoing and that it is with your best clients. That is everything I have. I have not seen one of your numbers, and I have not decided what is causing it.

So take this as one example of a way to look at the problem, not a fix for it. I am shooting in the dark here, on purpose, because I would rather put something concrete in front of you than send you another question.

Once I know the specific problem, I would build to that exact thing instead. It probably would not look much like this page.

Invented data

Every client name, amount and date on this page was made up to build the example. No real invoice, client or account was used, and none is needed to run it.

One question, asked of every invoice: how many days between sending it and having the money. Nothing here judges why a given invoice took as long as it took. Approvals, disputes, rework and plain slow payment all land in the same column, as days on the clock.

The pattern this ledger has

The two clients who bring in the most money are also the two who take the longest to pay. Copper Ridge Partners and Halyard Group are 60% of everything billed, and they average 52 days to cash against 31 days for everybody else. That gap is the whole problem in one number, and it is the reason a problem like this stays open. The accounts you would chase hardest are the accounts you least want to chase.

What moved

Copper Ridge Partners went from 46 days to 64 days over this ledger, which is 18 days slower on the largest account in the book. The smaller accounts barely moved. Nothing about a single invoice would have shown that.

  • An empty paid_date means the invoice is still open, and it gets counted as open through today.
  • Terms come from the gap between issued_date and due_date, per invoice, so mixed terms are fine.
  • Most accounting tools export exactly these columns already, usually as an invoice list or an aging detail report.
